Most relationships don't break over one big thing. They drift, quietly, during whatever stretch of life happens to be the hardest one — a new job, a loss, an illness, a new baby, a move, money stress that won't let up. Nobody decides to stop being partners. It just gets harder to find the time and energy that being partners actually requires, and something else slowly takes its place.

That something is usually logistics. You still talk — about the schedule, the bills, who's picking up what — but less and less about anything underneath it. One of you starts keeping score, even if neither of you says it out loud: who did more, who sacrificed more, who gets to be tired. Whoever's carrying more of the invisible load starts to feel less like a teammate and more like a manager with an employee who doesn't report to results. Resentment builds in the gaps nobody has time to talk through. Affection and real conversation, the kind that used to come easily, start running on a deficit, because there's nothing left over at the end of the day to offer each other.

None of that means the relationship has gone wrong. It means two people are under real pressure, and the ordinary friction of that shows up as distance, short tempers, or the sense of being on separate teams when you used to be on the same one. Most couples move through a season like this at some point. The relationship isn't broken; it's just running on fumes.

At the same time, there's a difference between ordinary strain and a pattern that's settled in — resentment that doesn't ease once the pressure lifts, the same fight circling back with no resolution, one of you feeling consistently unseen in what you carry, or the two of you functioning more like co-managers of a shared life than two people who actually like being around each other. Those patterns don't mean it's too late. They mean the relationship needs more attention than it's been getting, before the distance becomes the default.

If you're not sure which category the two of you are in, that uncertainty is itself reason enough to talk to someone. You don't need a crisis to justify starting, and you don't need to arrive with an agreed-upon diagnosis of what's wrong between you — that's what the first conversation is for.
