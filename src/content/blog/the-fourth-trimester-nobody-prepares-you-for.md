Most of the preparation around having a baby is aimed at the baby — the nursery, the feeding schedule, the pediatrician. Almost nothing prepares you for what it does to the two of you. The first twelve weeks after birth get called the fourth trimester for a reason, and it isn't just the parent who's adjusting — it's the relationship. Two people running on no sleep, operating out of fear and love in equal measure, are also quietly renegotiating who does what, who's noticed what, and who feels alone in it.

A lot of what shows up in that window is ordinary, even though it doesn't feel ordinary while you're in it. One of you starts keeping score, even if neither of you says so out loud. Night wakings, laundry, the mental list of what the baby needs next — it rarely splits evenly, and the partner carrying more of it starts to feel less like a teammate and more like a manager with an employee who doesn't report to results. Resentment builds in the gaps nobody has time to talk through. Touch and conversation that used to come easily now happen on a deficit, because there's nothing left over at the end of the day to offer each other.

None of that means the relationship has gone wrong. It means two people are under more pressure, with less sleep and less time alone together, than at almost any other point in a relationship — and the ordinary friction of that shows up as distance, short tempers, or a sense of being on different teams when you used to be on the same one.

At the same time, there's a difference between the ordinary strain and something that deserves more support — resentment that doesn't ease once sleep improves, the same fight circling back with no resolution, one of you feeling consistently invisible in the load, or the two of you starting to function like co-parents sharing logistics rather than partners who actually like being around each other. Those patterns don't mean it's too late. They mean this particular stretch needs more than it's getting.

If you're not sure which category the two of you are in, that uncertainty is itself reason enough to talk to someone. You don't need to be in crisis to work on this together, and you don't need to come in with a diagnosis of what's wrong between you — that's what the first conversation is for. And if it helps: bring the baby. A session doesn't need to be one more thing you have to arrange childcare around.
