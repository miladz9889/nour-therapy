Most of the preparation around having a baby is aimed at the baby — the nursery, the feeding schedule, the pediatrician. Almost nothing prepares you for what happens to the parent. The first twelve weeks after birth get called the fourth trimester for a reason: the baby is still finishing a kind of development that used to happen inside you, and you are going through something just as disorienting, with almost no script for it.

A lot of what shows up in that window is ordinary, even though it doesn't feel ordinary while you're in it. Grief for your old life, even when you wanted this baby badly. Resentment toward a partner who gets to leave the house. An identity that feels like it's been rearranged overnight and hasn't settled into its new shape yet. Exhaustion that goes past tired into something closer to disorientation. None of that means something has gone wrong. It means something enormous has happened, physically and psychologically, in a very short time.

What I want people to know is that the hard parts are allowed to just be hard, without it meaning you're failing or ungrateful or broken. You're allowed to love your baby and also mourn who you were before. Both at once, no contradiction.

At the same time, there's a difference between the ordinary hard and something that deserves more support — persistent feelings of dread, a sense of disconnection from the baby that doesn't ease with rest, intrusive thoughts that frighten you, or a flatness that doesn't lift over weeks. Those aren't a personal failing either, but they are a sign that this particular stretch needs more than it's getting, and that more help should be in the room, not less.

If you're not sure which category you're in, that uncertainty is itself a good enough reason to talk to someone. You don't need to self-diagnose before you reach out — that's what the first conversation is for. And if it helps: bring the baby. A session doesn't need to be one more thing you have to arrange childcare around.
