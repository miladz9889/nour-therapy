Anxiety

When calming down isn't the goal

Samar Farange · Licensed Therapist, Seattle, WA · October 12, 2026

Almost everyone who comes to see me about anxiety starts with the same request, in one phrasing or another: make this feeling go away. I understand why. The feeling is awful — the racing thoughts, the tight chest, the sense that something bad is about to happen even when nothing is. Of course you want it gone.

But here's what I've noticed, both in the research and in the room: the harder someone tries to eliminate anxiety completely, the louder it tends to get. Fighting a feeling is itself a signal to your nervous system that the feeling is dangerous — which is exactly the message that keeps the alarm going. You end up anxious about being anxious, which is a much harder loop to climb out of than the original worry.

So I try to shift the goal, gently, early on. Not toward calm as the finish line, but toward capacity — can you have the anxious feeling and still make the phone call, still go to the thing, still say the hard sentence to your partner. That's a different kind of win than feeling nothing. It's also a more honest one, because feeling nothing was never actually on the table.

In practice this looks like widening the gap between a feeling showing up and a decision getting made. Anxiety is very persuasive in the moment — it tells you the stakes are higher than they are, that you need to act now, that waiting is dangerous. Most of the time none of that is true. The work is building just enough space to notice the urgency, name it as the anxiety talking, and choose anyway.

None of this means your anxiety is irrational or that you should just push through everything. Some anxiety is correctly pointing at a real problem — a relationship that needs attention, a job that's genuinely untenable, a boundary nobody's enforced. Part of the work together is sorting out which is which, since the strategy is different depending on whether the alarm is accurate or just loud.

If this is familiar — the exhausting effort of trying to think your way out of a feeling that won't leave — it's worth talking about. That's what the first call is for.
